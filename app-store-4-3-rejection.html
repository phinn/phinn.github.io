App Store Rejection 4.3: Anatomy of a Batch Rejection (43 Apps, Two Strikes, Hard Numbers)

A field account from an indie iOS developer: 43 apps submitted in one month, 21 rejected in the same minute — twice. This is the full anatomy: triggers, the survey, what recovery realistically looks like (including the apps that didn't come back). Every number below comes from App Store Connect records and Resolution Center threads.

If you just got hit with Guideline 4.3(a) — Design - Spam (often bundled with 4.3(b) and 4.2.6), and especially if you got multiple rejections at once with a 9-question survey, this page is for you.

TL;DR — what actually triggers batch 4.3 rejections

  1. The trigger is submission velocity and account profile, not code similarity. I have a controlled experiment proving this (§2).
  2. A same-day batch rejection is an account-level flag. Every new submission will now be manually scrutinized until you clear it.
  3. The 9-question survey has a survival pattern: honesty + one app every 48 hours + lead with your best app.
  4. Hard budget rule that kept me safe ever since: ≤5 new apps per rolling 14 days, ≤8 per rolling 30 days.

1. What happened to me

Before October 1st: 28 apps on the account, every submission approved normally. Then I went into burst mode — 43 new apps inside one month, peaking at 3 submissions on October 5th.

At 5:25 AM on October 6th, Apple answered everything I had submitted that week:

  • 21 apps REJECTED simultaneously — 54% of my account
  • All 21 rejection letters carried timestamps within the same minute
  • Every letter cited 4.3(a) Design - Spam, most bundled with 4.3(b) and 4.2.6 Minimum Functionality
  • Six apps got the additional 9-question survey (the "are you a template factory" interrogation)

Why did my account look like a factory? Honest self-audit: 10 apps used rhyming suffixes (-let/-ette families), 24 apps shared one support-page template (same h1, same CSS, same footer), and four-language metadata shipped in lockstep. An reviewer doesn't need to open Xcode — the App Store search page alone exposes the pattern.

2. The experiment: it's not the code

During the flag window, three queues ran simultaneously on my account:

Read row three twice. An app sharing no code with anything on the account got the same spam survey on day one — while older apps built from the same skeleton sailed through updates in the same window.

Batch 4.3 is triggered by submission velocity and account-level profile — not by code fingerprints.

This changes your whole playbook: reskinning won't save you, refactoring won't save you. Pacing is the only lever.

3. The survey: answering the 9 questions

The survey is Apple's "prove you're not a template factory" step. Every question fishes for mass-production evidence. Four disciplines decided which apps came back:

  1. Be honest. When asked whether apps share code or assets — say yes, and explain what (engineering skeleton, localization pipeline) and how each app differs functionally. Two inconsistent answers across apps = account-level trust collapse.
  2. One reply every 48 hours. Never batch. Sending 21 copies of the same answer is a signed confession that you are exactly what they're asking about.
  3. Lead with your best app. The first one you answer should be your strongest: real usage, full feature set, complete differentiation materials. Its approval paves the way for the rest.
  4. Rewrite every answer per app. Mechanism over marketing: which APIs, why, what user value.

Result: most of the batch came back — roughly three weeks from first honest reply to final reinstatement. But this is where I have to be honest about what recovery does not look like: as of today, 6 apps from the second strike remain frozen, 0 have been restored. A 4.3(b) freeze is not a rejection you can re-appeal; it has sat through multiple 48-hour-cycle replies with no change. Treat any "frozen" state as potentially permanent when you plan your pipeline.

4. The relapse (so you don't repeat it)

After recovery, I pushed volume again. Second verdict: 6 apps frozen, cooldown of 14+ days. The difference: the first time I could say "I didn't know." The second time, I was the reason. The risk model doesn't give credit for past recovery — it only reads the current window's velocity.

About the Extended Review warning: an app carrying that text is on a watchlist. Correct move is not to resubmit quickly — document your remediation in Resolution Center, fix the real underlying issue, and wait out the cooldown. Apps with 2+ rejections need human-level handling, not pipeline treatment.

5. The rules I run now

  1. Rolling 14-day new-app budget ≤ 5; 30-day ≤ 8. Check before every submission. This is the only mathematical guardrail.
  2. No rhyming naming families. Ask: if an reviewer sees all my app names together in search results, what conclusion do they reach?
  3. Support pages written per-app — independent h1, independent structure. No template clones.
  4. Zero competitor trademarks in metadata — title, subtitle, keywords. I ate a 4.1(c) Copycats rejection on an old app's routine update for this.
  5. 48-hour cadence, single-thread, never batch.
  6. Freeze all new submissions while any 4.3 is unresolved.
